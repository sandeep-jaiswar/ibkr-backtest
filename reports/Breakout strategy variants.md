# Capped winners and wide stops undo the breakout edge

Our 20-bar breakout keeps a **66% win rate** but its winners average **+5.8%** against **-8.9%** losers, a payoff of 0.66. At that payoff the strategy breaks even only at a **60% win rate**, so the 1.25 profit factor rests on about six points of margin, and in 2026 the win rate fell to **41%**. The diagnosis points at the exits and the fills more than the entries: no tested variant touched the fixed profit target, which closes **261 of 398 trades at +5.8%**. The literature gives no direct support for any proposed change on NSE daily breakouts. The ATR stop has design precedent but lost to baseline on our data (1.17 vs 1.25), and the volume, breadth and time-stop changes have no supporting evidence, with two of them lowering profit factor. The baseline is also optimistic: the backtest has no price-band data, counts locked days as fills, and the 1.25 is the best of several tuned configurations. The highest-value next steps are a fill model that respects circuits, a frozen out-of-sample period, and a trailing exit in place of the fixed target.

## Where the strategy loses money

The arithmetic is simple and unforgiving. Each trade's expectancy is roughly 0.66 × 5.8% − 0.34 × 8.9%, or about **+0.74% per trade before costs** (our arithmetic, assuming the reported averages are gross). Delivery costs of about **22 bps per round trip** plus 5 bps slippage take roughly 0.27 to 0.32 points of that, which leaves about 0.4 to 0.5% net, so **costs consume around 40% of the edge**. The cost figure is consistent with the statutory schedule in our BSE work, where a delivery round trip comes to about **0.224%** before slippage (project file, `reports/BSE 500 universe and data access.md`). Whether the reported averages are already net of costs is not stated in our results and needs confirming.

The exit mix explains the payoff. A 2R target on an 8.9% average stop would sit near +18%, yet the average target exit is +5.8%, so most trades exit at a closer measured-move level. That is the main reason winners are small. The stop is also wide: a realized average loss of -8.9% against a 10% cap means most stops sit at the wide end of the allowed range. In rupee terms a 2 lakh slot loses about Rs 17,800 on a stop and gains about Rs 11,600 on a target, and with five slots fully deployed there is no cash buffer. The slot cap is a second leak. Of roughly 1,634 signals, **1,236 (about three in four) were skipped** for lack of a slot or a failed filter, and our notes show no ranking rule decided which signals got the slots. The 2026 result, 32 trades at a 41% win rate and -17%, sits well below the 60% break-even. Under a true 60% win rate, 13 or fewer wins in 32 trades has roughly a 2% chance (our normal approximation), so the drop is unlikely to be pure noise, though 32 trades cannot separate a regime change from a bad stretch.

## Which changes have evidence

Our own variants already tell us more than the literature does. Every variant except the time stop stays above 1.0, but none beats baseline, and the baseline was selected from the same data after several rounds, so this comparison favours it. The table lists each variant with its evidence.

| Variant | Profit factor (full span) | Win rate | Evidence found | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline (support-low stop, 2R or measured move) | 1.25 | 66% | Project result | Reference, optimistic |
| V1 ATR stop 2x | 1.17 | 39% | Turtle and Chandelier design precedent, secondary sources only; a Dow 30 ATR-stop backtest reports PF about 1.34 with unconfirmed parameters ([Liberated Stock Trader](https://www.liberatedstocktrader.com/atr-indicator/)) | Lower PF on our data |
| V2 volume 1.5x | 1.21 | n/a | Vendor and practitioner claims only; a vendor test found standalone breakout volume "useful" in 44% of cases ([JumpStartSignal](https://jumpstartsignal.pages.dev/signals/breakout-volume/)) | No gain; fewer trades |
| V3 breadth 50% regime | 1.05 | n/a | None | Worst filter change |
| V4 time stop 15 bars | 0.98 | n/a | None found | Below 1.0; likely cuts winners that need time (inference) |
| V5 ATR 2x with 5% cap | 1.20 | n/a | None beyond V1 | Close to V1 |
| V6 ATR + volume + breadth | 1.16 | n/a | None | No synergy |
| V7 V6 + time stop 15 | 1.18 | n/a | None | Time stop helps here but hurt alone |

The ATR result is more informative than its profit factor suggests. Because profit factor equals win rate × payoff ÷ (1 − win rate) × loss, V1's 1.17 at a 39% win rate implies a payoff near **1.8**, against 0.66 for baseline (our arithmetic, assuming the same trade set). The ATR stop cut losses sharply and gave up win rate, and the net effect on profit factor was negative. The literature supports the direction of this trade-off only in theory. The Turtle rules use a **2N (ATR-20) stop** with 20-bar entry and 10-bar exit ([TrendSpider](https://trendspider.com/learning-center/richard-dennis-turtle-trading-strategy/), secondary), and Zarattini, Pagani and Wilcox (2025) report that a trailing ATR stop on new highs makes **fewer than 7% of trades drive cumulative profit** ([Harbourfront Quant summary](https://harbourfrontquant.substack.com/p/does-trend-following-still-work-on), secondary; the paper was not read). That finding argues for letting winners run, which our fixed target prevents. None of our variants tested that change.

Relative-strength ranking has the most support of any idea here, but only in the cross-sectional momentum literature: a third-party replication of Clenow's approach reported **12.3% a year against 5.2% for the S&P 500** over 1999 to 2014, while a forum replication was skeptical ([The Chartist](https://www.thechartist.com.au/?p=5829); [Quantopian archive](https://quantopian-archive.netlify.app/forum/threads/stocks-on-the-move-by-andreas-clenow)). That work rebalances monthly or weekly, not on daily breakouts. Reward-to-risk ranking has no evidence. The time stop and the breadth regime have none either. The volume and stop-width findings in the notes are practitioner claims, and the one hit-rate figure found for volume is a vendor "consistency" score, not a win rate.

The broader academic record is mixed to negative for the rules we are using. Brock, Lakonishok and LeBaron (1992) found buy signals beat sell signals on Dow data from 1897 to 1986, but they report no profit factor and the result is contested ([Econpapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:47:y:1992:i:5:p:1731-64)). Bessembinder and Chan (1998) argued that costs offset those profits ([Bohrium abstract](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/simple-technical-trading-rules-and-the-stochastic-properties-of-stock-returns/813313297775329282-10567)), and Hudson, Dempsey and Keasey (1996) found UK rules had predictive ability but no excess return after costly trading ([Econpapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jbfina:v:20:y:1996:i:6:p:1121-1132)). Park and Irwin's survey of 95 studies finds profitability is weakest on daily stock data in later periods ([Econpapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jecsur:v:21:y:2007:i:4:p:786-826)), which fits a strategy whose edge may have decayed. Indian evidence is thin: Muruganandan (2020) finds technical rules on the Sensex fail to deliver consistent abnormal returns, but tests MACD and RSI, not breakouts ([CBJ](https://mgmt.cmb.ac.lk/cbj/index.php/testing-the-profitability-of-technical-trading-rules-across-market-cycles-evidence-from-india/)). **No study we found reports a profit factor or win rate for NSE daily breakouts.** Every literature claim above is unverified in our own data, and most rest on abstracts or secondary summaries.

## Indian execution the backtest does not model

The backtest has no price-band data, and this is the largest gap between the results and what a trader could have done. NSE sets daily bands for non-F&O stocks from a periodic list that is revised every few months, and secondary summaries report **2%, 5%, 10% and 20%** bands (the NSE price-bands page could not be fetched, so this rests on the Zerodha FAQ and circular summaries, [Zerodha](https://support.zerodha.com/category/trading-and-markets/trading-faqs/general/articles/what-does-circuit-limits-i-e-price-bands-mean)). F&O stocks have no fixed circuit but a dynamic operating range of about 10%, though one broker list says F&O names have no circuit filter at all, so the source conflicts. Orders outside the band are rejected, and NSE's limit price protection rejects buy limits above an LPP ceiling ([NSE LPP FAQ](https://nsearchives.nseindia.com/web/sites/default/files/inline-files/FAQs_on_Limit_Price_Protection.pdf), read through a search snippet). The practical effect is that a stock hitting its upper circuit has buy orders queued and few sellers, so a breakout buy may never fill ([Mastertrust](https://mastertrust.co.in/blog/upper-circuit-and-lower-circuit-in-the-share-market-explained)). A stop-sell on a lower-circuit day can likewise go unfilled and the next open can gap through it ([Kotak](https://www.kotaksecurities.com/support/how-is-it-possible-for-the-price-to-hit-the-market-but-the-stop-loss-sl-was-not-triggered)).

Our backtest does none of this. It fills breakout entries at the breakout price, fills stops at the stop price, and does not record locked days as no-fill. That biases the baseline upward in two ways. The strongest breakouts are often the ones that lock at the upper band, so the backtest banks wins that could not have been bought, and on the lower side it fills stops that would have gapped or never traded. The net direction of the bias is not known without band data. Band history must also be point-in-time, because the band assigned to a stock changes with each revision, and our notes found no historical per-scrip band dataset. The 10% cap on stop distance is also not the same as a tradable exit when a stock sits in a 2% or 5% band. Stop distance and band width interact, and the backtest treats them as independent.

F&O and non-F&O names behave differently for our purposes. A non-F&O stock on a 5% band can take several sessions to move 10%, so a stop can be triggered late. An F&O stock has a wider effective range but is also the stock where dynamic bands and LPP are most likely to matter. Our 269-symbol universe is not classified by band or F&O status, so the two groups are pooled. Settlement adds a constraint the model should reflect: cash settles T+1 and the sale proceeds should not be reused until the next day under a conservative assumption ([SEBI](https://www.sebi.gov.in/sebi_data/attachdocs/oct-2025/1761825190101.pdf) on optional T+0 for qualified brokers, from our BSE notes).

## How much to trust the 1.25

The baseline is a selected winner, not a first guess. The configuration was chosen after several rounds, and each round tested a set of variants on the same 2015 to 2026 data. The reported profit factor is therefore the maximum over that search, and the expected out-of-sample figure is lower. Bailey and co-authors argue that holdout methods are unreliable for investment backtests and propose a probability of backtest overfitting that counts every configuration tried ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2326253), abstract only). We cannot compute that correction yet because our notes do not record how many configurations were tried, so the count itself is the first thing to reconstruct.

Survivorship is a second, separate problem. Our 269-symbol universe is "after data screen," and the notes do not state whether the list is today's listings or a point-in-time membership. The BSE work in our project shows what the risk looks like: a current 500-name snapshot is survivor-biased by construction, and no public point-in-time archive was found (project file, `reports/BSE 500 universe and data access.md`). If the NSE list is also a current snapshot, failed and delisted names are missing, and the failures are exactly what a breakout stop must absorb. The data screens add further selection. The price cap was removed after it wrongly excluded high-priced stocks, but a range rule (high above four times low) still falsely excludes YES Bank's real March 2020 crash. If that rule is applied across the whole sample rather than bar by bar, it is look-ahead and removes the stocks that collapsed. One bar in ADANIENT's 2015 history is still unresolved. Each of these pushes the baseline's profit factor up, and none of them is measured here.

## Next steps, ranked by value

The ranking below weighs how much each step could change the sign of the result against what it costs. Steps one and two come before any further tuning, because tuning on a biased fill model only fits the bias.

| Rank | Step | Why it ranks here |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Make fills band-aware: no fill for entries at the band, stops on lower-circuit days unfilled with gap-through at the next open, point-in-time band history from NSE circulars | Could move the baseline's sign; the notes give no band data, so this needs NSE archive access |
| 2 | Freeze a holdout: choose settings on 2015 to 2020 and report 2021 to 2026 once; reconstruct the count of configurations tried and report a deflated or PBO-style figure | Sets how much the 1.25 is worth; 2026 is the early warning |
| 3 | Test a trailing exit in place of the fixed target (Chandelier 3 × ATR(22), close-based), keeping the 10% cap | The only untested lever on the payoff diagnosis; literature support is secondary |
| 4 | Rank signals by relative strength (for example six-month return rank) under 1 to 5 slots, against first-come selection | Uses the 1,236 skipped signals directly; momentum evidence is moderate and monthly, not daily |
| 5 | Sweep stop width (ATR 1.5, 2, 3 and the 5% and 10% caps), reporting win rate, payoff and expectancy in R together | Shows whether the ATR result is a stop effect or a cap effect; the 5% cap variant is the clue |
| 6 | Diagnose 2026 month by month against the index trend and the stop and target mix, without adding filters | 32 trades cannot support a new filter; the diagnosis should come first |
| 7 | Test relative volume and delivery percentage as filters | Low priority; evidence is practitioner or absent, and V2 gave no gain |
| 8 | Drop the time stop and breadth regime unless new evidence appears | Both lowered profit factor and have no literature support |

## Conclusion

The most useful reframing is that this strategy is a **payoff problem wearing a win-rate costume**. A 66% win rate looks healthy until it is set against a 60% break-even, and our variants mostly moved the payoff and the win rate in offsetting directions without net gain. The fixes we tested were entry filters and stop geometry, while the fixed target and the slot cap, which together shape most of the P&L, went untested. The honest state of the evidence is that no source supports a specific NSE daily-breakout rule, the baseline is a tuned winner evaluated on a universe with unknown survivorship, and the fill model overstates what a trader could have captured on circuit days. Until the fill model and holdout are in place, a change in the variant comparison of a few hundredths of profit factor is within the noise these caveats leave open.
